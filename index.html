Value

Why We Pay?

The first time I priced my tarot readings, I stared at my handwritten price list for a long time.

From an economic perspective, the cards themselves are not worth much. They are simply pieces of printed paper, with a production cost of only a few dollars at most. Yet I carefully assigned a price to every service: single-question readings, relationship readings, yearly forecasts, and more.

Later, as a consumer myself, I began to notice something interesting.

For most ordinary products, price seems to follow a certain logic. Higher production costs often lead to higher prices, while lower costs usually result in lower prices. At least in my understanding, this principle applies to most goods.

But there are always exceptions.

Luxury goods, for example.

When I was younger, I often wondered why a handbag could cost thousands of dollars. After studying microeconomics, I learned about branding, scarcity, and market barriers. I realized that price does not necessarily reflect cost.

Then I started reading tarot for other people, and I found myself on the other side of the transaction.

That was when I began to see how complicated the relationship between price and value really is.

A tarot card costs almost nothing to produce. Yet I knew that the people who came to me were never paying for the card itself.

What they were paying for might have been the opportunity to be heard, a chance to organize their thoughts, or simply the comfort of having someone sit with them through uncertainty.

The same card can hold completely different meanings for different people.

For someone who does not believe in tarot, it is just a piece of paper with illustrations on it. For someone struggling with uncertainty, it may represent reassurance, insight, or even the courage to move forward.

At that moment, I began to realize that value may not belong to an object itself.

Value exists in the relationship between people and objects.

Because of that, the same thing can hold entirely different value depending on who is looking at it.

If value is subjective, then does the same principle apply to people?

If value depends on the observer, then if this is true for objects, could it also be true for human beings?

I started thinking about myself.

For years, I have been searching for my own value.

I worry about grades. I worry about college applications. I compare myself to people who seem to have things I do not. More often than not, I find myself relying on external measures to determine my worth.

But if the value of a tarot card changes depending on who holds it, perhaps the value of a person changes as well.

The version of me seen by my parents, my friends, my teachers, and myself may never be exactly the same.

That realization led me to another question.

If value is relational, then whose standards should I use to define myself?

My grades?

My college decisions?

The praise and recognition I receive from others?

Or my own?

I still do not have a complete answer.

But I have started to believe that human value is not a fixed number.

It shifts with relationships, experiences, and circumstances.

Just as the same tarot card can carry different meanings in different hands, people are constantly interpreted through different lenses.

And perhaps learning how to understand my own value—not through rankings, scores, or other people's judgments, but through the connections I build with the world—is why I find the idea of value so fascinating.

Choices

Why We Choose?

Life is full of choices.

Some are small, like deciding what to eat for breakfast or which product to buy when shopping. Others are much bigger, like deciding whether to leave home and study abroad—a single decision that can shape an entirely different version of my life.

I've always thought choices were complicated.

At first, choosing seemed simple. You compare two options and pick the better one.

But the more I paid attention, the more I realized that people compare very different things depending on what they're choosing.

When we buy something, we often compare prices. A cheaper option usually feels like the better deal.

When someone comes to me asking whether they should break up with their partner, I can list the pros and cons of staying or leaving. But even after every argument has been laid out, they still have to make the choice themselves.

Because this time, they're not comparing prices.

They're choosing between logic and emotion. Between letting go and holding on.

And that's what makes those decisions so painful.

The people who struggle the most with choices are often the ones who understand the situation perfectly. They're not confused because they don't know enough. They're confused because they care.

Eventually, I started noticing something interesting.

The standards people use to make decisions change depending on what matters to them.

When we buy products, we compare prices.

When we think about relationships, we compare what we gain and what we lose.

And when it comes to college, I began to see something even more complicated.

When I was in tenth grade, my host brother was going through the college application process.

The months leading up to application season are the exact stage of life I'm entering now.

Back then, he was rarely home on weekends.

Later, I found out why.

His parents were constantly taking him to visit different colleges.

What surprised me was how different their approach was from what I had grown up seeing.

In the environment I came from, college choices were often tied to rankings.

Higher rankings meant better schools.

More prestige meant better opportunities.

Sometimes it felt as if students were choosing a name rather than a place.

To get into those schools, people competed over everything: AP classes, SAT scores, GPA, research projects, competitions, and extracurricular activities.

To be honest, I was part of that competition too.

My host brother, however, seemed much happier.

He was an athlete, played for his school team, and spent a lot of time doing something he genuinely loved.

His grades were decent, but what stood out was how confident he seemed in his own direction.

His family wasn't trying to find the highest-ranked college possible.

They were trying to find the right one for him.

And that made me wonder.

If higher rankings automatically mean better choices, why do people willingly turn down schools with stronger reputations?

If everyone is looking for the best option, why do different people end up choosing completely different paths?

The more I thought about it, the more I felt that people are rarely choosing between options themselves.

More often, they're choosing between what those options represent.

For some families, a college represents prestige, resources, and social recognition.

For others, it represents personal growth, belonging, and happiness.

They're looking at the same list of schools, yet making completely different decisions because they value different things.

That was when I started questioning something I had always assumed.

Is choosing really about finding the best option?

Or is it about finding the option that aligns most closely with what we care about?

If that's true, then how do we define a "good" college in the first place?

Are the prestigious schools that many Chinese students dream about objectively better?

Are the colleges carefully chosen by American students somehow worse?

Eventually, I realized that much of my confusion came from searching for a universal standard.

I wanted to know which school was better.

Which decision was more correct.

Which path was more worthwhile.

But life isn't a math problem.

Different people define "better" in different ways.

And when our values differ, our choices naturally differ too.

Maybe the hardest part of making decisions isn't figuring out which option is best.

Maybe it's figuring out what matters most to you in the first place.

Culture

Why We Belong?

During the summer after tenth grade, I traveled to a small village in Guizhou to film a documentary about Buyi Opera, a traditional art form of the Buyi ethnic group.

Before joining the project, I had never even heard of the Buyi people.

As a Chinese student, that realization felt embarrassing.

Everyone in China learns that there are fifty-six officially recognized ethnic groups, yet very few people can actually name all of them, let alone understand their cultures.

I had always considered myself someone who cared deeply about Chinese culture, yet I knew almost nothing about many of the minority cultures that exist within China itself.

That made me wonder:

Why are some cultures widely known while others slowly disappear from public attention?

Why do some traditions survive while others struggle to be passed on?

The village we visited was beautiful, but it felt completely different from the world I had grown up in.

I had seen crowded business districts filled with skyscrapers and bright city lights, but I had never seen a quiet mountain village.

I had spent years riding escalators and elevators in shopping malls, but I had never walked along muddy mountain roads.

I had met countless office workers rushing through their daily routines, but I had never met people who were trying to preserve a cultural tradition while simply trying to make a living.

During one interview, we asked members of the opera troupe why fewer young people were learning Buyi Opera.

The troupe leader's answer was simple.

"Young people leave the village to find work. The people who stay are mostly children and the elderly. Before culture can survive, people have to survive."

It was a practical answer.

A painful one, too.

Culture depends on people.

When people leave, traditions struggle to survive with them.

For communities facing economic hardship, preserving culture often comes after meeting more immediate needs.

I couldn't stop thinking about what he told us.

For the first time, I began to see how closely culture and economics are connected.

Later, I found myself thinking about Japan.

Historically, many aspects of Japanese culture were influenced by China. Yet Japanese culture is often more visible on the global stage.

Why?

Part of the answer, I suspect, lies in economic development.

A strong economy gives culture the resources, platforms, and opportunities to be seen.

But my questions didn't stop there.

Long after leaving Guizhou, I kept thinking about something else.

Maybe culture doesn't disappear when a tradition is forgotten.

Maybe it disappears when nobody is left to tell its story.

Culture doesn't only exist in museums or textbooks.

It exists in language, customs, memories, and the way people understand themselves.

When young people leave their hometowns, they don't just take their labor with them.

They also carry pieces of their culture.

And that made me wonder:

If I left the place where I grew up, what would I carry with me?

I've always loved Hip-Hop culture.

Anyone familiar with Hip-Hop has probably heard the phrase:

"Don't forget where you're from."

For me, those words have always meant more than simply remembering where I came from.

They mean remembering the place that raised me.

I've lived in the United States for three years now.

During that time, I've encountered stereotypes, prejudice, and hostility toward Asians and Chinese people—sometimes in real life, sometimes online.

But I've never felt ashamed of where I come from.

Not of my ethnicity.

Not of my language.

Not of my identity.

Because where I come from is more than a location on a map.

It is another force that raised me, alongside my parents.

The language I speak, the values I hold, the way I understand family, culture, and community—all of these were shaped by the place where I grew up.

They didn't appear out of nowhere.

They came from my environment, my experiences, and the people around me.

The place I come from helped shape who I am.

And it continues to shape who I am becoming.

Guizhou taught me that culture needs economic support in order to survive.

Living in America taught me something else.

Culture doesn't only shape communities.

It shapes individuals too.

We travel.

We learn new languages.

We meet new people.

But no matter how far we go, we carry traces of where we came from.

Whenever someone asks me,

"Where are you from?"

I never have to think about my answer.

"I'm from Guangzhou, China. If you don't know, it's a city near Hong Kong.

Stories

Why We Tell Stories?

I've noticed something interesting about the people who come to me for tarot readings.

Most of them aren't really looking for answers.

Or maybe a better way to put it is this: they already have their answers.

One of my friends once came to me for a reading.

He was studying in Australia and told me that he wanted to fly to Singapore to see his ex-girlfriend. He wanted to get back together with her and asked me what would happen if he went.

I still remember the cards I pulled that day.

They weren't particularly encouraging.

I told him honestly that I didn't think things would end well, and that he would probably leave feeling hurt and disappointed.

He disagreed.

He told me they had spoken on the phone recently and that her attitude seemed positive. He genuinely believed reconciliation was possible.

The way he spoke made it obvious that no tarot card was going to change his mind.

So instead of trying to convince him otherwise, I pulled a few advice cards, gave him some suggestions, and encouraged him to follow his heart.

As expected, things didn't go the way he hoped.

When he arrived in Singapore, she met him once, rejected his attempt to get back together, and told him that his persistence was only making her feel pressured.

That night, he called me several times from his hotel room.

He cried and asked what he could do to make her change her mind.

Should he keep trying?

Should he buy a ticket and leave the next day?

I knew that no objective advice would help him at that moment.

So I asked him a question.

"If you could go back and do it all again, knowing this would be the outcome, would you still come to see her?"

He had been drinking a little and wasn't entirely sober.

But his answer came immediately.

"Of course."

"Even if nothing changed, I would still come."

"I just wanted an answer."

I didn't pull any more cards for him.

I simply said:

"That's enough."

"If it's something you truly want to do, you don't need to know the outcome beforehand."

After that conversation, I began thinking about something.

People are always searching for answers.

Or perhaps what they're really searching for is an oracle.

The people who come to me for tarot readings are searching for one.

People struggling with relationships are searching for one.

People who feel lost about their future are searching for one.

We all want someone to tell us what will happen next.

We want someone to tell us which path is right.

We want someone to tell us who we are supposed to become.

The problem is that the world doesn't work that way.

There are very few absolute answers.

In Daoist philosophy, there is a belief that when something reaches an extreme, it begins to transform into its opposite.

Misfortune can become fortune.

Strength can become weakness.

Everything contains the possibility of change.

Tarot feels the same way to me.

My clients often want certainty.

They want a definite answer.

But certainty is the one thing I cannot give them.

The best I can offer is possibility.

A probability.

A direction.

Never a guarantee.

Because there are no completely bad cards, just as there are no completely good ones.

The Death card may represent endings and loss, but it also represents renewal and transformation.

The Tower may symbolize disaster and upheaval, but it can also represent rebuilding and growth after collapse.

Even the most feared cards often contain hope.

To me, that reflects the idea that destruction can become creation.

And the Lovers card, one of the most desired cards in relationship readings, is no exception.

Beyond romance and connection, it can also represent dependency, imbalance, and losing yourself in another person.

Even the cards people love most carry their own warnings.

The longer I studied tarot, the more I realized that what fascinated me wasn't tarot itself.

It was uncertainty.

Because who can truly provide the answers to life?

My life feels like a tarot spread too.

And even I can't predict what comes next.

When I was younger, I loved watching My Little Pony.

Every pony had a Cutie Mark that represented their talent and purpose.

Only Twilight Sparkle spent a long time searching for hers.

I remember thinking she seemed lost.

Looking back now, I think I was searching too.

As a child, I was always wondering what my dream was.

I wondered who I would become.

And eventually I started asking an even bigger question:

If everyone dies in the end, then what is the meaning of life?

Now that I'm eighteen, I think I finally have my own answer.

My dream isn't grand.

It's actually very ordinary.

I want to live this life as fully as I can.

I want to cherish the people I meet.

I want to experience everything life places in front of me.

I want to allow myself to feel every emotion that comes with being human—not just happiness, but sadness, fear, disappointment, love, hope, and everything in between.

I want to understand the world more clearly.

And I want to understand myself more clearly too.

I don't really want to become anyone else.

When I was younger, I imagined becoming Anne Hathaway simply because I thought she was beautiful.

I imagined becoming Bill Gates because he was rich.

Sometimes I imagined becoming a political leader because I wanted the power to change things and solve people's problems.

But now, I only want to become myself.

A version of myself that is sometimes sensitive and vulnerable, sometimes stubborn and resilient, but always genuine.

As for the meaning of life—the question that once felt impossible to answer—I think I finally understand my own response.

We spend so much time assigning meaning to things.

To relationships.

To objects.

To achievements.

To our lives.

But perhaps many things don't come with meaning attached to them.

Perhaps existence itself is enough.

The meaning of life isn't finding a perfect answer.

It's living.

Experiencing.

Feeling.

Loving.

Doing the things that matter to you.

By the time I reached that conclusion, something else became clear.

The oracle I had been searching for was never in the cards.

It wasn't fate.

It wasn't destiny.

And it wasn't hidden inside some perfect answer about how life should be lived.

It was always within me.

A quiet voice.

Sometimes uncertain.

Sometimes fragile.

But always there.

And if life truly has an answer,

perhaps that answer is simply being yourself.
